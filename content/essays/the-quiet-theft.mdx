There is a feeling I used to have when writing — a particular friction, like dragging a thought up from somewhere deep and dark and half-formed, and slowly forcing it into language. It was uncomfortable. It was slow. It was, I now understand, mine.

I don't know exactly when that feeling started to change. But somewhere in the last two years, I noticed it getting quieter. Not absent — but softer, shorter, easier to skip. I started reaching for autocomplete before the sentence was done. I started drafting with AI assistance before I had fully decided what I wanted to say. I started using the machine not as a tool for finishing thoughts, but as a shortcut for beginning them.

I don't think this makes me unusual. I think this makes me a person living in the current moment.

But I've been sitting with a question I can't quite shake: what are we losing when thinking gets easier?

---

## The interiority problem

Cognition researchers have a term for the feeling of your own mind working: metacognition. It's the experience of thinking about thinking — the internal sense of effort, confusion, insight, and revision that constitutes genuine intellectual engagement. And there is growing evidence that outsourcing cognitive effort doesn't just change what you produce. It changes what you experience while producing it.

This isn't a new worry. We've had versions of it about calculators, GPS, spell-check, search engines. The critique is always the same: if the tool does the hard part, you lose the development that comes from struggle.

What's different now is the scope and intimacy of the substitution.

A calculator replaces numerical computation. A GPS replaces spatial navigation. But a large language model that can draft your emails, outline your essays, argue the other side of your position, and synthesize your research doesn't just replace a specific cognitive function. It replaces the rough first contact with ideas — the messy, effortful moment before you know what you actually think.

That moment is not a bug in human cognition. It is, arguably, the point of it.

---

## What authorship actually is

I want to be careful here. I'm not arguing that AI assistance is cheating, or that using language models is intellectually dishonest, or that all output produced with AI help is somehow fake. That's too simple, and it doesn't match how I actually use these tools.

What I'm arguing is narrower and stranger: that the subjective experience of authorship — the feeling that a thought came from you, was struggled for by you, is genuinely yours — is not a trivial side effect of thinking. It is, in some sense, what thinking is for.

Identity is largely constructed through what we believe we have done, what we believe we have chosen, what we believe we have decided and discovered and created. The narratives we tell ourselves about our own minds are not decorative. They are structural.

When you outsource the generation of an idea to an AI, even if you then edit and shape and revise the result, something subtle shifts in the felt ownership of the output. The idea doesn't feel as fully yours — and that feeling, trivial as it sounds, has consequences for how you relate to it, how you defend it, how much you actually believe it.

---

## The gradient, not the cliff

The challenge is that this shift happens on a gradient, not a cliff. Nobody decides one morning: *I will stop thinking for myself*. It happens in a thousand small accommodations.

You use AI to help articulate a feeling you couldn't quite name. That's fine — useful, even. You use it to structure an argument you've already formed. Still fine. You use it to draft a first paragraph when you're blocked. Also fine. You use it to generate the idea you were supposed to have on your own. Now you're somewhere different — but you arrived there through steps so incremental that no single one felt like crossing a line.

This is the texture of most technological capture. It doesn't feel like surrender. It feels like convenience compounding.

The question isn't whether any single instance of AI assistance changes who you are. The question is what happens to the accumulated practice of effortful cognition when the friction is slowly removed from the process. What happens to a muscle that is never exercised.

---

## Cognitive inequality, quietly

There's a political dimension here that doesn't get enough attention.

Access to AI tools — and more importantly, the literacy to use them critically rather than passively — is not evenly distributed. The educated and the economically comfortable are more likely to use AI as a tool for augmenting their own thinking. The less educated and the economically precarious are more likely to use it as a replacement for effort — not because they're less intelligent, but because they have less time, less access to good models, less context for evaluating outputs critically.

This creates a new axis of inequality. Not just who has access to information, but who retains the cognitive practices that allow them to evaluate and question information independently. The people who can afford to use AI slowly and critically, who have enough background knowledge to catch its errors and hallucinations, who have enough time to revise and push back — those people get augmented.

Everyone else gets subtly outsourced.

---

## What I'm not saying

I'm not saying we should ban AI from thinking. I'm not saying the friction of struggle is good for its own sake, the way ascetics value discomfort. I'm not saying the tools are evil.

I'm saying that what they take from us is less visible than what they give, and that the invisible subtraction deserves honest attention.

I'm saying that the experience of laboring over a thought, of not knowing what you think until the sentence is done, of discovering your own position through the effort of articulating it — that experience is worth naming before it becomes historical.

I'm saying that the question *what does it feel like to think?* is not a soft question. It's the question.

And I'm saying that the answer is changing, quietly, right now, and most of us are participating in that change without fully deciding to.
