The inequality discourse around AI is almost entirely economic. Who will lose jobs. Which industries will be disrupted. Whether the gains will be broadly distributed or concentrated at the top. Whether we need a universal basic income. Whether retraining programs are possible or delusional.

These are real questions. They matter. I'm not dismissing them.

But there is another inequality forming — quieter, less legible, potentially more consequential — that almost nobody is discussing seriously: cognitive inequality.

Not who has access to AI. Who retains the capacity to think without it.

---

## Two ways to use a tool

When a genuinely powerful tool enters a society, two patterns emerge. One group learns to use it as an amplifier — it enhances what they already bring to it. Another group uses it as a replacement — it substitutes for capacities they either haven't developed or have allowed to atrophy.

This isn't about intelligence. It's about preparation, access, and the structural conditions that shape how people engage with new technology.

A doctor using AI to catch rare diagnoses she might otherwise miss is being augmented. She brings decades of medical training and clinical judgment; the AI adds a layer of pattern-matching that she uses critically and selectively. The output is better medicine.

A student who uses AI to write every essay they're assigned, never developing the capacity to argue a position through sustained prose, is being substituted. They might produce acceptable essays. They are not developing the cognitive apparatus that essay-writing was supposed to build.

The difference is not the tool. It's what the person brings to the tool — and what happens to their underlying capacities over time.

---

## Education and the cognitive floor

Education systems, at their best, build a cognitive floor. Not just knowledge, but the practices of thought: making arguments, evaluating evidence, holding complexity, sitting inside difficult questions, producing sustained analytical prose.

These practices are effortful to acquire. They require years of deliberate difficulty. They are, in many educational systems, already under-taught or under-resourced.

AI enters this already-unequal system and further stratifies it.

Children at well-resourced schools, with teachers who understand what AI can and cannot do, can be taught to use AI tools critically — to interrogate outputs, to use it for research rather than replacement, to understand where it fails and why. Their cognitive development is potentially enhanced.

Children at under-resourced schools, with teachers who are overwhelmed, with curricula focused on testing rather than depth — these children are more likely to use AI as pure shortcut. The path of least resistance. The substitute for the hard work nobody has time to teach them to do.

Twenty years from now, the difference will be visible. One group will have the cognitive practices that allow them to operate in an AI-mediated world with genuine agency. The other group will be dependent on AI in ways they don't fully understand and can't fully control.

---

## The epistemological problem

There's a specific cognitive capacity that AI threatens in an unusual way: the ability to evaluate whether information is true.

This matters because AI is very, very good at producing plausible-sounding, well-structured, confidently-stated content that is completely wrong. Language models hallucinate. They reproduce biases at scale. They can be prompted to argue any side of any debate with equal apparent authority.

The filter for all of this is human judgment — specifically, domain knowledge and epistemic practices developed through years of reading, learning, and thinking.

If you have enough background knowledge in a field, you can catch AI errors. If you don't, you can't. If you've developed strong reading habits and critical thinking, you'll notice when something sounds wrong. If you haven't, the plausibility of the output will be enough.

This is not a technology problem. It's a prior-knowledge problem. And prior knowledge is unequally distributed in exactly the ways you'd expect.

---

## The political consequences

I want to be careful about being too deterministic here. Inequality in prior knowledge doesn't automatically translate to political inequality. History is full of politically effective movements built by people who were educationally underserved by the systems around them.

But in an information environment increasingly saturated with AI-generated content — content that can be produced at zero marginal cost, can be personalized at scale, can be targeted with extraordinary precision — the capacity to evaluate information critically is a form of political power.

People who can read an AI-generated news summary and think: *what is this leaving out, who wrote this, what sources is this drawing on, what's the argument structure here, where does this fail?* — those people are less manipulable. They have more genuine agency in their political formation.

People who don't have those tools are more vulnerable. Not stupid — vulnerable. There's a difference. The vulnerability is structural, not personal.

---

## What would help

I don't have a clean answer here. Inequality at this scale is structural, and structural problems don't have individual solutions.

But some things seem relevant: an educational culture that treats critical thinking and analytical writing not as luxuries for the advanced track, but as fundamental literacies for everyone. A public conversation about AI that moves beyond jobs and into cognition. AI literacy programs that are genuinely critical rather than just technical — that teach people what AI can't do, not just how to use it.

And, more than anything, a recognition that the cognitive capacities at risk here aren't decorative. They are the infrastructure of agency. The ability to think for yourself, to evaluate what you're told, to produce original arguments — these are not soft skills. They're the difference between citizenship and dependency.

We should be worried about the second kind of AI inequality. We're not worried enough.
