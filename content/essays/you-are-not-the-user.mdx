The word "user" is interesting. It implies agency — someone who uses a thing. But the platforms that built the modern internet were not, primarily, designed for the people who use them. They were designed for the people who pay for them. And the people who pay for them are advertisers.

This is not a conspiracy. It's not even a secret. It's the stated business model of almost every free platform you interact with daily. Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, Twitter, Google — these companies' customers are not you. You are what they're selling.

You know this. Everyone knows this. "If you're not paying for the product, you are the product" has been a truism for long enough that it's lost its teeth. But I want to push on it, because I think knowing it intellectually and actually understanding what it means for your inner life are very different things.

---

## What it means to be designed against

When a platform is optimized for advertiser outcomes, it is — structurally, technically, deliberately — optimized to shape your behavior in ways that serve that outcome. This means your time on platform. Your emotional state while on platform (anxious people scroll more). Your willingness to click. Your openness to influence.

The UX of every major platform is not designed to give you a good experience in any humanistic sense. It's designed to give you an experience that makes you maximally useful to advertisers. These are not the same thing. Sometimes they overlap. Often they don't.

Consider: a platform that made you feel genuinely satisfied and complete after 20 minutes of use would be a failure by every metric it is actually optimized for. What you want — genuine connection, useful information, occasional entertainment — and what advertisers want from you are structurally in tension. The platform resolves that tension in one direction every time.

---

## The feedback loop

What makes this more disturbing than mere commercial cynicism is how quickly the designed behavior becomes internalized. Platforms don't just influence your behavior on them. They slowly reshape what you want, what you find satisfying, what register of emotion feels normal.

The dopamine loop of social validation — likes, comments, shares, follower counts — is not a natural feature of human social life. It is an interface design decision, made by engineers, optimized for engagement. But after years of use, it stops feeling artificial. It starts feeling like what social connection is.

People raised with these platforms don't experience social approval as something ambient, cumulative, and private. They experience it as a number. A public number. That they check. Multiple times a day.

This is not a neutral change in how social life is mediated. It's a profound restructuring of what social approval means, how it feels, and what you do to get it. And it happened because someone decided that quantified public validation would be good for engagement metrics.

---

## The outrage economy, specifically

The attention economy runs on emotion. Specifically, on strong emotion. And the strongest, most reliable, most engagement-generating emotion available to platform designers is outrage.

Not joy. Not wonder. Not even sadness. Outrage.

This is not speculation. The research is robust. Negative emotional content spreads faster, generates more comments, holds attention longer, and keeps users on platform more than neutral or positive content. Platforms that optimize for engagement are, by structural logic, platforms that optimize for anger.

So when you scroll a timeline and feel yourself becoming exhausted, anxious, and vaguely hostile toward everyone — that's not an accident or a side effect. That's the product working as designed.

The question is what it does to you, over years, to have your emotional environment architected around the reliable production of outrage. What it does to your politics. Your capacity for nuance. Your ability to hold uncertainty. Your basic tolerance for people who disagree with you.

I don't think the answer is pretty.

---

## AI enters this system

Here's where it gets more complicated: AI is now entering this system, and not from the outside.

AI-generated content is increasingly flooding the same platforms optimized for engagement. AI-powered recommendation systems are becoming the hidden arbiters of what you see. AI chatbots are being deployed by the same companies that built the attention economy, with the same underlying business incentives.

The question is whether AI will disrupt the attention economy or be absorbed by it. Whether language models that can generate infinite content will break the outrage loop by flooding it, or will be trained on engagement data and simply learn to produce the most outrage-inducing content automatically.

Early signs are not reassuring.

---

## Where this leaves you

I'm not going to tell you to delete your apps. That advice is both obvious and structurally useless — the alternatives are almost as captured, and total withdrawal from platforms has its own social and professional costs.

What I think is more useful is a clearer-eyed understanding of the design relationship you're in every time you open an app. You are not using a service built for your benefit. You are inside a system that was built to extract value from your attention and behavior, on behalf of clients who are not you.

This doesn't mean you can't get genuine value from these platforms. You can. But you're doing it in a hostile environment, against the grain of the design, despite what the system wants from you.

Understanding that changes the relationship. At least a little. At least as much as understanding can change anything, which is less than we'd like but more than nothing.
